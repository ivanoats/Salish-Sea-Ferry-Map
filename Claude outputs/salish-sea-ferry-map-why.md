## README section ("Why this exists")

I'm a transportation nerd, and I went looking for a good map of ferry routes around the Salish Sea — car ferries and passenger-only, every operator, one map. It doesn't exist. WSF publishes its own routes, BC Ferries publishes its own, and so does everyone else — Black Ball, Kitsap Transit, the Victoria Clipper, Skagit County, Whatcom County — each on its own site, in its own format. If you want to see all your options at once, you piece it together by hand.

I plan a lot of bike-and-ferry trips — this map exists so I can look at a loop like Kingston to Bainbridge Island and see every leg of it, across every agency, at a glance.

It's also drawn at the scale I actually think in. I see this region as the Salish Sea before I see it as Washington or British Columbia, and the map follows that: one shape, one border-crossing sea, not a set of state and provincial fragments.

## Blog post ("Why I Built a Map of the Whole Salish Sea Ferry System")

I'm a transportation nerd. Given a good transit map, I will look at it for longer than is reasonable. So when I started planning a bike trip that involved hopping a ferry from Kingston to Bainbridge Island, I went looking for a map that showed me the whole ferry network around the Salish Sea — car ferries and passenger-only boats, every operator, one map, so I could actually see my options.

It doesn't exist. Washington State Ferries has a map of Washington State Ferries. BC Ferries has a map of BC Ferries. Black Ball, Kitsap Transit's fast ferries, the Victoria Clipper, the little county-run boats out to Guemes and Lummi Island — each one lives on its own site, drawn to its own operator's boundaries. If you want to see the whole system, you're stitching it together yourself, tab by tab.

That bugged me enough to build it myself: one map, 37 routes, every ferry operator running in the Salish Sea, drawn as one connected system instead of a pile of agency-specific fragments.

Part of this is just practical — I want to look at a route like Kingston–Bainbridge and see every leg of the trip at once, not reconstruct it from six different websites. But part of it is bigger than trip planning. I don't think of this place as Washington-plus-BC with a border running through it. I think of it as the Salish Sea — one body of water that happens to have a national boundary drawn across it, not the other way around. That's not just a personal quirk of mine; it's closer to how the Indigenous peoples of this region have understood it for a very long time — the sea as the center of life here, not the edge of anything.

So this map draws the region the way I actually see it: one sea, connected by boats, regardless of which country or agency runs which route.
